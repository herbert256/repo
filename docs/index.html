How to use this book

No plant in this book has ever grown. Each is raised from its plate number alone — the same plant for every reader, on every day — and engraved afresh, line by line, whenever its page is opened.

  • ← → turn the pages  ·  R open at random
  • S hear the plant’s song — it keeps singing as you turn the pages
  • Gather pollen on one plate, turn to another and pollinate it to raise a hybrid. Hybrids can be crossed again.
  • Click a plate while it is being engraved to finish it at once.
  • The frontispiece is Flora herself, goddess of flowers, engraved after Titian — she sings too.

Flora

Dea florum et veris

vulg. Chloris; the Mother of Flowers; the Lady of the Floralia

❧

To Flora, goddess of flowers and of the flowering of all things, this herbarium is dedicated — as is only proper, since every plant in it is, in the end, her doing, and she has never once been asked to account for any of them.

Ex Ovidio

She was once the nymph Chloris, of the Fortunate Isles, until Zephyr, the west wind, saw her, pursued her and carried her off — and then made what amends a wind can: he married her, and gave her a garden in the fields that were her dowry, where the air is always mild and something is always in bloom. “Chloris eram, quae Flora vocor,” she told Ovid — “I was Chloris, who am now called Flora” — and he had the presence of mind to write it down. By her own account it was she who first scattered new seeds among the countless nations of the earth; who turned the blood of Hyacinthus, of Narcissus and of Adonis into flowers; and who gave Juno the blossom from the fields of Olenus by whose touch she conceived Mars, without troubling Jupiter in the matter.

Floralia

Her games, the Floralia, opened on the twenty-eighth of April and ran on into May, and were by common consent the merriest days of the Roman year. The people put off their white for bright colours; hares and roe-deer, not lions, were let loose in the Circus; chickpeas and beans were flung among the crowd; and the actresses of the mimes, at the crowd’s loud insistence, performed without their clothes. When the stern Cato once sat among the audience, the people were too ashamed to make the customary demand in front of him — so Cato, being told of it, rose and left the theatre, that the goddess might be honoured in the usual way. The Fathers of the Church were scandalised for several centuries afterwards. Flora, by every account, was not.

Effigies

The likeness opposite is engraved after the picture by Titian, painted at Venice about 1515 and now in the Uffizi at Florence — a city which, some say, was named for her. Who sat for it is not known; Venice, which is rarely wrong about such things, said a courtesan. She offers a posy of roses, violets and jasmine with her right hand, and with her left holds her rose-coloured mantle to her — though not, it must be said, very closely.

Virtutes

Invoked for the blossoming of trees, of vines and of grain, for honey, and for youth — which, she held, should be enjoyed while it is in flower: monet aetatis specie, dum floreat, uti. The thorn, she adds, is despised once the roses have fallen.

Cantus

Her song is in F — the deep rose of her mantle, by Scriabin’s table — in the Lydian mode, the brightest of the old modes; it goes in a lilting six-eight, on the harp, over a drone like a bee in a warm room.
